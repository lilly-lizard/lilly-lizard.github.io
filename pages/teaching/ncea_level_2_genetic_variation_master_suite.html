🔗 Linked Genes & Crossing Over

What are Linked Genes? Genes located close together on the same physical chromosome. Unlike unlinked genes, they do not undergo independent assortment and tend to be inherited together as a block.

Breaking Linkages: The only way to separate linked alleles is through Crossing Over. When homologues align in Prophase I, non-sister chromatids form chiasmata and exchange segments. This separates linked alleles, creating recombinant gametes.

Chiasma Frequency Rule: The physical distance between linked genes determines how often they are separated. Genes far apart are frequently separated by crossing over. Genes located extremely close to each other are rarely separated.

🧬 Sex-Linked Inheritance Patterns

What is Sex Linkage? Genes located on the sex chromosomes (usually the larger X chromosome) that control non-sexual traits, such as red-green color blindness or hemophilia.

Why Males are Biased: Females inherit two copies of the X chromosome (XX), meaning a recessive disease allele on one X can be fully masked by a healthy dominant allele on the other (heterozygous carriers).

Males, however, have only one X chromosome (XY). Because they are hemizygous, they have no second allele to mask a mutant recessive allele. As a result, males express X-linked recessive disorders with far greater frequency than females.

Core Concepts Reference Guide

Topic 1 Mutations and the Gene Pool

Mutations are permanent, irreversible changes in the base sequence of DNA. They are the only source of brand new alleles in a species. They can be somatic or gametic:

  • Somatic Mutations: Occur in non-reproductive body cells (e.g. skin, organs). They only affect the individual and are not passed on to offspring.
  • Gametic Mutations: Occur in germline cells or gametes (sperm/egg). They can be inherited by offspring, introducing new alleles to the gene pool of the next generation.

Topic 2 Meiotic Variation Mechanics

Meiosis shuffles existing alleles into unique combinations across gametes via three processes:

  • Crossing Over (Prophase I): Homologous chromosomes align side-by-side. Non-sister chromatids form chiasmata and exchange structural blocks. This breaks linkages and builds recombinant chromatids.
  • Independent Assortment (Metaphase I): Homologous chromosome pairs align randomly at the cellular equator. Which maternal or paternal chromosome goes to which side is completely independent of other pairs. This creates 2ⁿ combinations.
  • Segregation (Anaphase I & II): Spindle fibers separate homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids, forcing allele pairs to separate so each gamete ends up with exactly one allele per gene locus.

Topic 3 Inheritance Patterns

Genotype-to-phenotype ratios are altered by several distinct dominance mechanics:

  • Complete Dominance: The dominant allele fully masks the expression of the recessive allele in heterozygotes (3:1 ratio in heterozygote crosses).
  • Incomplete Dominance: Neither allele is fully dominant. Heterozygous individuals express a blended, intermediate phenotype (e.g., RR red, WW white, RW pink flowers; 1:2:1 ratio).
  • Co-dominance: Both alleles are dominant and independently, fully expressed in heterozygotes (e.g., roan cattle showing both red and white hairs; 1:2:1 ratio).
  • Multiple Alleles: More than two alleles exist at a single gene locus within a population (e.g. human ABO blood groups with alleles Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i), though any individual inherits only two.
  • Lethal Alleles: An allele pairing (typically homozygous recessive) that causes death in the womb, eliminating that phenotype category and shifting the live-born Mendelian ratio to 2:1.

Topic 4 Population Genetics & Evolutionary Forces

The total collection of alleles in a breeding population is the gene pool. Allele frequencies fluctuate due to:

  • Genetic Drift: Random changes in allele frequencies due to chance events, especially active in small populations.
  • Founder Effect: A tiny, unrepresentative pioneer group migrates and establishes a new colony, carrying a biased subset of the parent gene pool.
  • Bottleneck Effect: A severe, catastrophic event randomly collapses the population size, killing off massive numbers of individuals and permanently destroying genetic variation.
  • Natural Selection: Selective pressures (e.g. climate, predators, disease) favor advantageous phenotypes, increasing the frequency of helpful alleles.
  • Gene Flow: The exchange of alleles between populations via migration (immigration/emigration).
