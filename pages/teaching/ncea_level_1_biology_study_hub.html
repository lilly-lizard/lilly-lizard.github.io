Unit Summary

Understanding Genetic Variation

Demonstrate understanding of genetic variation in relation to an identified characteristic. Learn how DNA structure leads to inherited traits, how sexual reproduction and mutation generate variation, and how selective pressures alter population gene pools in Aotearoa New Zealand.

01

DNA, Genes & Chromosomes

Source & Nature of Variation

  • Nucleotide Structure: Deoxyribose sugar, phosphate group, and nitrogenous base (A, T, C, G).
  • Complementary Base Pairing: Adenine pairs with Thymine (A-T), Cytosine pairs with Guanine (C-G).
  • Genetic Code: Triplet codons code for specific amino acids, forming functional proteins.
  • Mutations: Permanent changes in DNA base sequences that produce new alleles.
02

Reproduction & Selection

How & Why Variation Occurs

  • Meiosis vs Mitosis: Mitosis produces 2 identical diploid somatic cells; Meiosis produces 4 unique haploid gametes.
  • Sources of Variation: Independent assortment, crossing over, and random fertilisation.
  • Genotype to Phenotype: Homozygous dominant, heterozygous, and recessive monohybrid ratios (3:1, 1:2:1).
  • Natural Selection: Favourable traits improve survivability and reproductive success.
03

Gene Tracking & Pedigrees

Inheritance Methodologies

  • Pedigree Charts: Tracking inherited traits and genetic disorders across human or animal generations.
  • Genetic Markers: Using specific DNA sequences to determine carrier status or relatedness.
  • Changing Environments: Population bottleneck, small population size, migration, non-random mating.
  • Conservation Applications: Preserving genetic diversity in native species like kiwi and kakapo.

Topic 1 DNA Structure, Alleles & Heritability

An individual organism carries a genetic code stored inside the nucleus of its cells. The physical structure of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) is a double-helix formed by repeating units called nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of three main components: a sugar molecule (deoxyribose), a phosphate group forming the sugar-phosphate backbone, and a nitrogenous base.

DNA Hierarchy Analogy

  • • Base Sequence / Codon: Individual letters & words
  • • Gene: A single recipe coding for a specific functional protein
  • • Chromosome: A complete recipe book (humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs)
  • • Genome: The entire library in the cell nucleus

Heritable vs Acquired Traits

  • • Heritable Traits: Determined by DNA base sequences passed from parents via gametes (e.g., eye colour, blood type).
  • • Acquired Traits: Result from environmental influences and life experiences (e.g., dyed hair, large muscles from weightlifting, sunburn). Acquired traits cannot be passed on to offspring because they do not alter gamete DNA.

Mutations: A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA base sequence. Mutations are the ultimate source of all new genetic variation because they create entirely new alleles (alternative forms of a gene). If a mutation occurs in somatic cells (body cells), it affects only that individual. However, if a mutation occurs in gametes (sperm or egg cells), it can be inherited by the next generation.

Topic 2 Cell Division, Inheritance & Natural Selection

Sexual reproduction increases genetic variation within a population through cell division and fertilisation processes.

Mitosis vs Meiosis

Mitosis: Produces 2 identical diploid body cells (46 chromosomes in humans) for growth and repair. No variation generated.

Meiosis: Produces 4 genetically unique haploid gametes (23 chromosomes in humans) for sexual reproduction.

Key Meiosis Variation Factors

  • • Independent Assortment: Homologous chromosome pairs align randomly at the cell equator, yielding unique combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in gametes.
  • • Crossing Over: Homologous non-sister chromatids exchange segments of alleles during prophase I, creating new allele combinations.
  • • Random Fertilisation: Any sperm can fertilise any egg, producing millions of potential unique diploid zygotes.

Natural Selection & Gene Pool: In any population, environmental selection pressures (e.g., disease like kiwifruit PSA, predators attacking kiwi chicks, or climate shifts) act upon the individual phenotypes. Individuals possessing advantageous phenotypes (coded for by favourable alleles) have higher survivability and reproductive success. They pass these favourable alleles on to their offspring via gametes, causing an increase in the frequency of that allele in the population gene pool over successive generations.

Topic 3 Gene Tracking, Pedigrees & Changing Populations

Pedigree charts and DNA markers are tools used by geneticists and conservation biologists to track the inheritance of characteristics across generations.

Pedigree Symbols

Squares represent males, circles represent females. Shaded shapes indicate affected individuals possessing the studied phenotype.

Monohybrid Ratios

Heterozygous cross (Aa x Aa) yields 3:1 dominant to recessive phenotype ratio, and 1:2:1 genotype ratio (1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa).

Population Dynamics

Small population sizes (bottlenecks), migration, and non-random mating significantly alter genetic diversity and allele frequencies.

A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA base sequence. This change alters the triplet codon order, producing a different amino acid sequence that creates a functional protein producing a new allele and phenotype (e.g. disease resistance). When a selective pressure such as PSA disease enters the environment, non-resistant plants die while resistant individuals survive. Because surviving individuals reproduce sexually, they pass on the favourable allele via their haploid gametes to the next generation. Over successive generations, the proportion of resistant individuals increases, resulting in an increased allele frequency for disease resistance in the population gene pool.

The IDEL Answer Framework for NCEA Biology

How to structure your written answers to reach Merit and Excellence grades.

1. Identify (Achieved)

Define the basic scientific term or state the direct observation (e.g. "A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA base sequence.")

2. Describe (Achieved+)

Provide details on what happens or what the biological process looks like (e.g. "Mutations in gamete cells can be inherited by offspring.")

3. Explain (Merit)

Explain HOW and WHY it happens (e.g. "Changing the base order alters triplet codons, creating a new amino acid order and functional protein.")

4. Link / Discuss (Excellence)

Connect multiple concepts into a logical chain across generations (e.g. "Survival advantage → sexual reproduction → passing allele via gametes → increased gene pool frequency.")

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • Don't confuse gene vs. allele: A gene is the section of DNA coding for a characteristic; an allele is the specific variation/version of that gene.
  • Don't forget gametes vs. somatic cells: Somatic cell mutations affect only the individual; only gamete mutations can be passed on to offspring.
  • Never say an organism "adapts to survive" in its lifetime: Individuals don't choose to mutate. Random mutations exist first; environmental pressures select for pre-existing beneficial phenotypes!
